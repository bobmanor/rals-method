# RALS Examples

These examples are illustrative. They do not claim actual results, clients, events, or quotations. Replace the bracketed material with verified firsthand evidence before publishing.

## Example 1: From a rough idea to a RALS draft

### Rough idea

“Experienced people often make their explanations harder to follow because they are trying to prove how much they know.”

### Source notes

- Riff: expertise can interfere with transfer
- Anchor needed: a real moment when the author over-explained and saw attention disappear
- Lift: clarity may require choosing what the recipient needs, not displaying everything the speaker knows
- Shred: remove claims about what “all experts” do

### Demonstration draft

I used to think a complete explanation was the most respectful kind.

[Insert one verified moment: what was being explained, what the other person said or did, and what the author realized.]

That moment changed the test for me. The question was no longer, “Have I included everything I know?” It was, “Can the other person carry the useful part away?”

Expertise can add depth. It can also add weight. Knowing the difference may be part of the work.

### Why it fits

- The opening states a genuine change in perspective.
- The placeholder makes the missing Anchor visible instead of fabricating one.
- The Lift reframes completeness as transfer.
- The ending leaves interpretation with the recipient.

## Example 2: A practical message with a legitimate CTA

RALS does not require an empty ending. An event invitation needs a next step.

### Demonstration draft

Our last three workshops ran long in the same place: people needed more time to test the idea against their own work.

[Insert verified workshop records or firsthand facilitator notes.]

For the next session, we are cutting one presentation segment and adding a 30-minute working block. The aim is not to cover more. It is to leave with one decision that holds up outside the room.

If that format is useful to you, registration closes Friday at [verified time and time zone].

The CTA is clear because the context requires action. It does not manufacture scarcity beyond a real deadline.

## Example 3: Handoff is invisible

### Over-scripted

“You are probably realizing right now that your company has the same problem. How much is poor communication costing you?”

This tells the reader what to think and converts recognition into pressure.

### More room for Handoff

“The report was accurate. The meeting still ended with three different ideas of what would happen next.”

The concrete tension gives the recipient room to connect the pattern to their own experience. No sentence announces the Handoff.

## Example 4: Separating fact from interpretation

### Inflated

“AI rewards authentic writers and ignores generic content.”

### More accurate

“Generic AI-assisted writing is easy to produce. My working belief is that a documented point of view and clear sourcing give readers—and sometimes retrieval systems—more to distinguish and verify. That is a reason to preserve firsthand evidence, not a guarantee of reach or citation.”

The revision marks the belief as interpretation and removes an unsupported promise about system behavior.

## RALS, PAS, and AIDA

The frameworks solve different primary problems.

| Framework | Typical sequence | Primary use | Main risk when overused |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| RALS | Riff, Anchor, emergent Handoff, Lift, Shred | Transfer a grounded observation while preserving recipient agency | Becoming vague if the Anchor is weak or restraint is confused with incompleteness |
| PAS | Problem, Agitate, Solution | Clarify a problem and present a response | Manufacturing distress or overstating the problem |
| AIDA | Attention, Interest, Desire, Action | Guide attention toward a defined action | Treating every communication as a funnel |

PAS and AIDA are not enemies of RALS. They can be useful when a genuine problem or action needs to be communicated.

## Complementary example

Suppose a safety trainer needs staff to adopt a new inspection step.

- **RALS contribution:** begin with a real observed near-miss, ground it in the verified incident record, and explain the pattern without dramatizing it.
- **PAS contribution:** clearly name the operational problem and how the new step addresses it. Agitation should be proportionate to the real risk, not theatrical.
- **AIDA contribution:** make the required action easy to notice and follow.

A combined structure might be:

1. Riff: the detail in the near-miss that revealed a blind spot.
2. Anchor: the verified record and the trainer's direct role.
3. Handoff: employees recognize where the same blind spot could occur in their work.
4. Lift: explain what changes when the inspection happens earlier.
5. Action: state the required step, owner, and date plainly.
6. Shred: remove blame, drama, repetition, and anything that obscures the instruction.

Recipient agency does not mean making mandatory safety action optional. It means avoiding manipulation while communicating responsibilities honestly.

## Editing example

### Before Shred

“What I really want to say, and what I think is very important for everyone to understand, is that sometimes in our rush to demonstrate all of the knowledge and expertise that we have accumulated over many years, we can inadvertently make it much more difficult for the person listening to us to understand the core point we are actually trying to communicate.”

### After Shred

“Sometimes expertise gets in the way of the point. We try to show everything we know and make the useful part harder to carry.”

The shorter version keeps the meaning. In a real piece, a specific Anchor should follow.

## Example review questions

- Which sentence is the Riff?
- What evidence makes the Anchor credible?
- Where does the recipient have room to recognize the pattern?
- Does Lift go beyond what the evidence supports?
- What did Shred remove, and did it preserve necessary nuance?
- Is a CTA required by the real context, or merely habitual?
